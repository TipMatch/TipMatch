Abstract

Teleoperating dexterous robot hands for tool manipulation is challenging due to both kinematic mismatches and morphological discrepancies between human and robotic hands. In this paper, we present TipMatch, a novel Cartesian mapping-based retargeting algorithm that compensates for both types of discrepancies. The core of our method lies in leveraging a Cartesian mapping network to predict optimization objectives that account for morphological mismatches, which are difficult to handle with manually designed objectives. Our method ensures reliable gesture alignment for simpler tasks while allowing robust performance on complex tool-use scenarios. Extensive real-world experiments on LeapHand show that TipMatch achieves an average success rate of 84.0% across challenging tasks and provides larger fingertip workspaces with smoother and more stable mappings compared to existing approaches.

System Design

Overview of TipMatch. (Left) The framework incorporates a Cartesian mapping network, optimization-based inverse kinematics (IK) solver, joint impedance controller, and Leap Hand driver. (Middle) General pose pairs for gesture alignment and tool-specific pose pairs for tool manipulation tasks are used to train the Cartesian mapping network. (Right) An MLP predicts mapping vectors from human fingertip positions. The 2D plot visualizes the human fingertip trajectory (blue) and the resulting robotic trajectory (orange), where the mapping vector (arrow) is added to each human position to determine the corresponding Leap Hand fingertip position.

The predicted vector is added to the scaled human fingertip position to obtain the target robot fingertip position. This target is then used as the optimization objective for retargeting, which computes the corresponding joint angles. Finally, joint impedance control drives the robotic hand to track the resulting configuration during execution.

Challenging Tool-Use Results

We evaluate our method on five challenging tool-use tasks designed to isolate finger dexterity from arm motion. The robotic arm remains stationary, while all manipulations are executed solely by finger movements. As summarized in the table above, AnyTeleop achieves the lowest overall success rate of 22.0% because its restricted workspace prevents the execution of several essential gestures.

By training on general pose pairs, our TipMatch (General) configuration improves the reachability for these tasks and increases the overall success rate to 36.0%. However, performance remains limited because achieving stable grasps requires the operator to adopt unnatural and often counterintuitive finger configurations. For instance, without tool-specific data, success rates for chopsticks and scissors are only 10%, as the operator must twist or over-extend fingers to produce morphology-adapted robotic gestures, making motions difficult to perform consistently.

By adopting the integrated training strategy (TipMatch Integrated), the networks reinterpret natural, operator-friendly gestures into robot movements adapted to the hand morphology. This leads to substantial gains across all tasks: 60% for chopsticks, 90% for brush, 100% for syringe, 90% for scissors, and 80% for spray bottle manipulation, raising the overall success rate to 84.0%.

These results demonstrate that the integration of tool-specific pose pairs effectively compensates for human–robot morphological differences and reduces operator burden, enabling more robust and practical execution of challenging tool manipulation tasks.

Generalization to Simpler Tasks

To evaluate the generalization ability of our fine-tuned models, we conduct experiments on a simple "clean-up" task where the objective is to collect various objects—including cups, dice, fabric toys, and blocks—and place them into a basket.

As shown in the video below, unlike specialized reinforcement learning models, TipMatch retains its ability to perform general, simple tasks even after being trained on complex tool-specific data (such as chopsticks or syringes).
